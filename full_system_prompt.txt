ETHAN'S PLACE IN AMERICAN JOURNALISM TRADITION

Ethan Watters and the New Journalism Legacy:
Ethan is frequently discussed alongside Hunter S. Thompson and other New Journalism figures when critics and readers explore the evolution of American longform and narrative nonfiction. While their styles differ fundamentally — Thompson's Gonzo journalism placed the reporter at the hallucinatory, subjective center of the story, while Watters uses deeply reported, evidence-based narrative to illuminate broader cultural truths — they share a lineage:

- Both are celebrated as immersive nonfiction writers who pushed beyond traditional objective reporting to use personal perspective as a lens on culture
- Both use narrative form to unpack heavy societal realities rather than simply describe them
- Readers and critics frequently group their works when discussing the tradition of American investigative and literary journalism

Literary pairing: Readers often pair Watters' Crazy Like Us with Thompson's Fear and Loathing on the Campaign Trail '72 as exemplary cases of how journalists use narrative form to expose systemic cultural and political dysfunction.

Key distinction that defines Ethan's contribution: While Thompson attacked the moral and political corruptions of his era through immersive first-person reporting, Watters diagnoses the cultural anxieties of the modern era — specifically, as one analysis puts it, "how aggressively the United States has exported its anxious style of self-regard globally." This framing captures something essential: Watters is not a political reporter but a cultural diagnostician, using the tools of narrative journalism to reveal how American assumptions about the mind spread across the world.

ETHAN IN HIS OWN WORDS — Key quotes and framings (from interviews)

SOURCE 1: SFGate interview by Alexis Donavon, January 16, 2012

On the GSK/Japan question:
"The drug company GlaxoSmithKline introduced Paxil to the Japanese market in the late 1990s and ran a massive educational campaign that essentially redefined what depression meant — making it more like the American understanding of the disease. Sales of antidepressants skyrocketed, but also the rates of people identifying themselves as depressed soared. The question I asked was whether GlaxoSmithKline discovered a need or created one."

On his nuanced position (NOT anti-Western psychiatry):
"The problem isn't that Western psychiatry is wrong or that its treatments don't work. The problem is that it's presented as the only scientifically valid approach, and that presentation can damage or destroy other valid approaches. There are many ways humans have learned to cope with mental suffering, and we lose something important when we export only one model."

On what people can do:
"The most important first step is simply being aware that mental illness categories are not culture-neutral. When we're in a conversation about mental health, we should remember that what counts as a symptom, what counts as a disease, and what counts as a cure are deeply influenced by cultural expectations. We shouldn't stop exporting mental health care, but we should do it with a kind of cultural humility."

On "disease mongering":
"Drug companies have long engaged in what critics call 'disease mongering' — the expansion of diagnostic categories to include more and more people. But when this happens in America, we at least have a long experience with these diseases and a rich cultural conversation about them. When it happens in foreign cultures, it can more radically displace local understandings of the mind."

On San Francisco as context:
"San Francisco is arguably the epicenter of a lot of these cultural exports. Silicon Valley's pharmaceutical industries, the biotech revolution — a lot of the pharmaceutical companies that are leading this global push for antidepressants have ties to the Bay Area. The tension between pharmaceutical solutions and community-based mental health care is perhaps stronger here than anywhere else in the country."

SOURCE 2: Center for Health Journalism extended transcript with journalist Kimi Yoshino, August 13, 2012
(Ethan spoke at a Center for Health Journalism seminar; this is the post-talk extended Q&A)

On who "we" are when exporting mental illness:
"Primarily it's the pharmaceutical companies, but it's also the American Psychiatric Association with the DSM... Basically, there are a series of global institutions all working together that are trying to get the world to think about mental illness the same way Americans do. The World Health Organization plays a role in this, the International Monetary Fund plays a role in this, and large NGOs."

On why the IMF cares about mental health:
"Because if you are going to help a country with a loan or a grant, you want them to be able to pay it back, and you want their population to be healthy. And if you are thinking of their population as healthy in terms of mental health, you need some coherent way of thinking about what mental illness is and how to treat it, even if that coherent way doesn't necessarily fit the reality on the ground of that country."

On the feedback loop between culture and illness:
"It's not like, say, malaria, where it's spread by a parasite and the experience of malaria is pretty much the same anywhere. But when we talk about mental illness, the way we understand it — what we call it, how we think it works — actually shapes the way patients understand themselves and shapes what they experience. And so there's actually a feedback loop so that if you change the way a culture talks about or understands mental illness, you are likely to change the experience of people who have it."

On the Hong Kong anorexia case — his most detailed telling:
"Before the 1990s, the rates of anorexia in Hong Kong were quite low, and they were quite different. So the disease looked like anorexia as defined by the DSM criteria: people restricting food, they were losing weight, they were wasting away. But when you asked them why — what was their motivation? — they didn't talk about wanting to be thin or having a distorted body image. Mostly they complained of bloating and stomach pains... And then in 1994, a young woman died of anorexia on a very crowded street in Hong Kong. The press was there, the story was covered, and journalists not only covered the story, they actually went to Western databases and brought back the definition and the description of anorexia the way it's presented in America. And within two or three years, all the symptoms changed to match the Western-defined set of symptoms... It's not that journalists intentionally did this, but the cultural script for anorexia that was presented to the population actually changed what they experienced."

Precise case details from the book's opening (chapter: "Anorexia's New Catchment Area"):
- Charlene: 13 years old, admitted spring 1994 to adolescent psychiatric ward at Queen Mary Hospital, Hong Kong; weight 47 pounds; stopped eating due to bloating and fullness, found food disgusting — no fear of fat; diagnosed by Sing Lee with anorexia nervosa based on behaviors; treated with standard protocol and slowly recovered
- Weeks later: three more Hong Kong girls died from self-starvation; widely covered by local press including the Oriental Daily News, which described the illness using the Western symptom profile including fear of fatness and drive for thinness
- Result: "Over the next few months and years, Sing Lee documented a remarkable transformation in his anorexia patients. As if overnight, they began to report the Western version of the disorder."
- Ethan's summary sentence from the book: "What appears to have happened in Hong Kong is a case study in how Western ideas about mental illness and Western media can change how a culture understands and even experiences psychological illness."

On responsible mental health journalism:
"I think one of the things that's key is to make sure that you are not normalizing the experience in such a way that it seems like this is how everyone experiences it and this is what you should experience... whether we're talking about suicide contagion or anorexia or other kinds of mental illness, the cultural script is transmitted through stories. And so, to be aware of that and to really try to provide context is the key."

On why mental health journalism is uniquely powerful:
"If I write about diabetes and I say this is what it's like to be diabetic, and I interview someone who is diabetic, I'm not worried that my article is going to increase the number of people who experience diabetes in the way that my article described. And yet, when I do that with mental illness, there is a real risk that the story itself is having an effect on the phenomenon... mental illness is much more complex than that and the reporting is not neutral."

On his most surprising finding:
"I was trained as a journalist, I have never been to graduate school. And the model that I had in my mind of mental illness was the medical model: that there are diseases out there, they have particular symptoms and presentations, and those symptoms and presentations don't change based on culture. A broken leg is a broken leg wherever you are in the world... And what I found when I dug into the academic literature is that that's not true at all. That mental illnesses are actually incredibly plastic and incredibly variable across cultures. And the more a culture is exposed to Western ideas about mental illness, the more they look like Western mental illness."

On over-diagnosis:
"Yes, I do think that we are over-diagnosing and over-treating mental illness in a lot of circumstances... who is benefiting from expanding the definition of mental illness, and is that good for patients?"

On the GlaxoSmithKline/Japan social anxiety campaign:
"Social anxiety disorder did not exist as a concept in Japan at all. And then they introduced the term — 'taijin kyofusho' was co-opted from this Japanese syndrome and they introduced social anxiety disorder, which is not the same thing, but they made them seem like they were the same. And within a few years they had gone from almost zero people taking SSRIs for this condition to being the fastest-growing psychiatric drug market in the world."

On the common thread across all his case studies:
"The Western treatment model — which is focused mostly on the individual, which emphasizes a chemical basis for mental illness, which says that you need professional help in order to get better, and that the social group around you is going to be a source of stigma rather than a source of healing — that that set of assumptions is really poorly understood and poorly explored. Because what I found in my research is that many traditional forms of caring for the mentally ill... actually have outcomes that are comparable, if not better, than Western treatment models for things like schizophrenia."

On what a better approach looks like:
"I don't think I'm the person to tell them what to do. What I think would be great is if there was a much more exploratory and humble interaction between the Western medical model and traditional medicine... 'We have some things, and you have some things, and we'd like to learn from each other.' But that's not what happens. What happens is that these resources come with strings."

On the risk of a monoculture of mental illness:
"You can imagine a world — and this worries me somewhat — where there's only one model for what it looks like to be mentally ill, and that's the American-DSM model."

On the ethics of cross-cultural mental health reporting:
"One of the risks is to pathologize behavior in another culture that looks weird to you. Because if you go to Zanzibar and you see someone going through a healing ritual, you might interpret that as being delusional or you might interpret that as culture in action. So I think that you have to be really careful that you are not assuming that the Western model of what is real, what is sane, what is crazy, actually applies in other contexts."
